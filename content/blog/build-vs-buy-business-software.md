Every growing business eventually faces the question: do we build software ourselves, or buy something that already exists? There is no universal answer, but there is a sensible way to decide. This guide gives you a framework.

## The short version

- **Buy** when your need is common and a good product already solves it.
- **Build** when the software itself is part of what makes you different.
- **Combine** when a product covers most of it and you need some custom parts.

## Why buying is usually the starting point

Ready-made platforms have advantages:

- **Speed.** You can start in days or weeks.
- **Lower upfront cost.** You share development costs with other customers.
- **Proven.** Real users have found and fixed many problems.
- **Ongoing improvements.** The vendor keeps developing it.
- **Support.** Someone else maintains it.

For things like a CRM, messaging inbox or HR system, buying is usually sensible. The problems are common and the products are mature.

## When building makes sense

- **It is your competitive edge.** A unique pricing engine or workflow that customers value.
- **No product fits.** Your process is unusual and adapting to a product would harm the business.
- **Integration needs are extreme.** You need deep, custom links between systems.
- **Scale or cost changes the maths.** Very large usage can make custom cheaper over time.

## Weigh the real cost of building

The build cost is only the start. Include:

- Design, development and testing.
- Hosting and infrastructure.
- Security and updates.
- Bug fixing and support.
- Changes as the business evolves.
- The people needed to maintain it after the project ends.

A common rule of thumb is that maintenance over the years exceeds the initial build. Plan for it.

## Weigh the real cost of buying

- Subscription fees that rise with users or usage.
- Features you pay for but do not use.
- Limits on customisation.
- Dependence on the vendor's roadmap.
- Effort to migrate if you leave.

## A simple scoring approach

Rate each option from 1 to 5 on these questions.

| Question | Buy | Build |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How well does it fit our process? | | |
| How fast do we need it? | | |
| What is the total cost over three years? | | |
| Who maintains it? | | |
| How much risk are we taking? | | |
| Does it give us an edge? | | |

Discuss the scores with the people who will use it.

## The hybrid approach

Many businesses do best by buying a platform and adding custom pieces, such as an integration, a special report or an automation around their unique process. This gets you speed and fit at moderate cost. It is also how we recommend approaching many projects.

## Questions to ask before deciding

1. Is this problem common to many businesses?
2. Have we seen a product that solves 80 percent of it?
3. What would we do with the time saved by buying?
4. Who would own and maintain a custom system?
5. What happens if the key developer leaves?

## Avoid these traps

- **Building because it feels more "yours".** Own the outcome, not the code.
- **Buying without checking fit.** Test with real data first.
- **Underestimating maintenance.**
- **Customising a product so much that upgrades become painful.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Is custom software more expensive than a subscription?

Upfront, usually yes. Over many years the answer depends on scale, maintenance and how well the subscription fits.

### Can I start with a product and build later?

Yes, and it is often wise. It lets you learn what you really need before committing to a custom build.

### What if the product does not do everything I need?

Consider integrations and small customisations before building from scratch.
