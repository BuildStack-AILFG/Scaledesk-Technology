Choosing a technology partner is one of the most consequential decisions a growing business makes. The right one helps you move faster and avoid expensive mistakes. The wrong one leaves you with half-finished software and a budget that has disappeared. These steps help you choose with confidence.

## Step 1: Define the problem before the solution

Many projects fail because the brief describes a solution ("we need an app") rather than a problem ("we lose leads because follow-up is manual"). Write down:

- The business problem and who feels it.
- What success looks like, in measurable terms.
- Your constraints: budget range, deadline and systems that must be connected.
- What you have already tried.

A good partner will help sharpen this. A poor one will simply start quoting.

## Step 2: Decide what kind of partner you need

- **A product vendor** if an existing platform already fits most of your needs.
- **A services firm** if you need something built or customised around your process.
- **A mix of both**, which is common: a platform plus configuration and integration.

Read [build versus buy](/blog/build-vs-buy-business-software) to weigh the choice.

## Step 3: Check relevant experience

- Have they solved similar problems for similar businesses?
- Can they explain past work in terms of business results, not just technology?
- Are you able to speak to a past customer?

Be cautious about long logo lists without specifics. Ask what the work involved and what changed for the customer.

## Step 4: Evaluate how they think

In the first conversations, notice whether they:

- Ask about your business before proposing a solution.
- Challenge assumptions politely.
- Are honest about risks and trade-offs.
- Suggest starting smaller when appropriate.

Good partners make your project simpler, not bigger.

## Step 5: Compare proposals fairly

A useful proposal contains:

1. Your problem restated in their words.
2. A phased plan with clear deliverables.
3. Timelines and who does what.
4. What you must provide, such as access, content and decisions.
5. Costs, and what is included or excluded.
6. How changes are handled.
7. Support after launch.

Compare on these points rather than only on price. A much cheaper quote often leaves out things you will pay for later.

## Step 6: Understand ownership and access

Ask:

- Who owns the code, data and designs?
- Will you have access to accounts and repositories?
- What happens if the relationship ends?
- How is your data secured?

You should never be locked out of something you paid for.

## Step 7: Assess communication and process

- Who is your day-to-day contact?
- How often will you see progress?
- How are issues raised and resolved?
- Do they demonstrate working software early, not just at the end?

## Step 8: Start with a small paid pilot

If possible, begin with a limited, well-defined piece of work. It shows how the partner behaves under real conditions and limits risk on both sides.

## Warning signs

- Promises of guaranteed results without knowing your business.
- Pressure to sign quickly.
- Vague scope and unclear costs.
- No discussion of testing or support.
- Reluctance to let you speak to customers.

## Questions to ask

1. What problem do you think we are really solving?
2. What are the biggest risks to this project?
3. What would you build first, and why?
4. How will we measure success?
5. What happens after launch?

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I choose the lowest quote?

Not automatically. Compare scope, experience and support. The cheapest option can become the most expensive one.

### How long should the selection take?

For a modest project, a few weeks. For a larger one, allow more time for conversations, proposals and references.

### Do I need a technical person to choose a partner?

Not necessarily, but it helps to have someone who can ask informed questions. A good partner will explain things in plain language.
